What It Really Costs to Build a Log Home, Frame to Finish

A realistic look at where the money goes on a modern log home build — from site work and the shell to the finish costs most first-time builders underestimate.

By Daniel MarshUpdated Aug 11, 2026
Log home under construction — lead photo

Every log home budget starts the same way: with a number that turns out to be wrong. Not because anyone is lying, but because the number people usually quote — the per-square-foot cost of the log package itself — is a fraction of what actually shows up on the final invoice.

The package is the easy part to price. Site work, foundation, mechanicals, and finish work are where budgets actually move, and they vary enormously by region, terrain and how much of the labor you're doing yourself.

Site prep and foundation work

Site work and foundation costs are the first place log home budgets go sideways, especially on sloped or wooded lots.

Start with the site. Clearing, excavation, a well and septic system, and a driveway can run anywhere from a modest line item to a third of total project cost, depending on how remote and how sloped the lot is. This is the single most underestimated category in every budget we've reviewed.

“The package quote is the floor, not the estimate. Everything else is what actually determines whether you finish on budget.”

Once the shell is up, mechanicals — plumbing, electrical, HVAC, and increasingly a mini-split or geothermal system suited to a log envelope — typically run 15 to 20 percent of total project cost. Log homes have specific HVAC considerations around settling and air sealing that a standard framing contractor may not price correctly on the first pass.

Interior finish work in progress

Finish work — flooring, cabinetry, trim and fixtures — is usually the largest single category after the shell itself.

Finish work is where most overages happen, because it's also where the most decisions get made. Flooring, cabinetry, countertops, lighting, and trim carry the widest price range of any category, and it's easy to creep upward decision by decision without noticing the cumulative effect.

The realistic takeaway: budget the log package as your floor, not your target, and hold back at least 15 to 20 percent of total project cost as contingency before you break ground.
